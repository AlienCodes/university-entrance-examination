Why is it harder to learn a second language?

Good morning, everyone. In our last lecture, we discussed briefly first language acquisition. We have
learned that we are born to process and produce natural human language by looking at what we are exposed
_to, or what we create with the universal grammar principles that we are born with. [We also know that we
acquire our native language in a natural way. However, if you have had the experience of trying to master
a second language, no doubt you find it to be a challenge, uh, quite unlike your first language experience.
Because there are important differences between acquiring a first language and learning a second one. So in
today’s lecture, I will discuss why it is harder to learn a second language by comparing the differences and

similarities between these two ways of language processing: And I’ll finish by briefly mentioning a related
topic, that is language translation.

Now let’s come to the differences first. First of all, as everybody knows, anyone who is learning a
second language has already acquired a first language, right? [2]So the language mechanism in the brain has
already had certain linguistic parameters set, [4]such as word order in the first language, [2]Jand that makes

second language learning quite different. [3]What one needs to learn are the specific rules of the second

language, which is often called the target language. In second language learning, the first language tends to
serve as a model, and mistakes often result from taking words from the target language and putting them
together, because learners tend to apply rules from the first language. For example, if the first language is
English and the target language is Korean, a second-language learner might position the Korean verb between
the subject and the object of the sentence, using the English word order, rather than placing the verb after
the object, which is the correct Korean word order. Generally speaking, the greater the difference between
the first language and the target language, the more tasks are involved in learning the latter. What’s more,
second language learning can even be characterized as a gradual shift from the first language orientation to
the target language orientation.

OK, let’s come to the second point. As we all know, first language acquisition typically takes place in
early childhood, at a very young age. However, second language learning, especially in a classroom setting,
proceeds more quickly with adults and adolescence than with younger children initially, although at the end
of the day, [6]it is the younger child who will become more proficient in second language learning than ,

grownups. That is to say, even in second language learning, [5]age is also a major factor, as in first language

acquisition. ，
The third point I want to bring up here is that it’s apparent that [7]first language acquisition happens

without conscious teaching, you know, in a kind of natural way, as I mentioned just now. But the second

language learning generally does not. Numerous studies have shown that [8]/[9]a number of traits, such as

self-confidence, motivation, good self-image and low anxiety, can facilitate second language learning, but none
of these traits seem as important to first language acquisition.

OK, let’s move to the fourth point. Studies have shown that [10]the input that affects second language
learning is more complex than that in a first language acquisition context. What do I mean here? Now, let me

give you an example. If a second language learner has a classroom teacher who talks in the target language
at a quick rate, in complex sentences and about complicated matters, the learner will probably have a harder
time in the learning process. However, first language acquisition can proceed at the same rate, whether or not
adults simplify their language.

Now, the fifth point is that [11]practice is important for second language learning, but not so much for
first language acquisition. Even very quiet children, I mean, kids who don’t like to talk to people, can acquire
their first language at the ordinary rate, but that doesn’t work for a second language learner. For example, he
