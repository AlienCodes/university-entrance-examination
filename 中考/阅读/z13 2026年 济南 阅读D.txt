On a hot summer afternoon, cities and towns are usually hotter than the nearby countryside areas. This is not just a feeling. Some cities can be as much as 7°C hotter. Scientists call this the “urban heat island” effect (城市热岛效应).

This effect is closely connected with the materials used in city buildings, which usually take in large amounts of heat from sunlight during the day. Instead of cooling quickly after sunset, these materials slowly give out the stored heat into the air. As a result, cities remain warm for a lot longer. In certain situations, high temperatures can even be deadly.

To cut urban heat, many cities have turned to trees, which work like natural air conditioners in two main ways. First, they provide shade (树荫), preventing streets and buildings from heating up too much. Second, trees cool the air through a process called transpiration—water inside the leaves enters the air, lowering temperatures nearby.

To understand how effective trees really are, researchers studied air temperature and tree cover data (数据) from nearly 9,000 cities, and the findings were surprising. Trees reduce the “urban heat island” effect by almost 50% all over the world. Since cities are usually 1–3°C hotter than nearby countryside areas, trees can lower temperatures by about 0.5–1.5°C in many places, which can have an influence on city heat during the long hot summer.

However, scientists warn that planting trees alone cannot completely solve the problem. Even with large planting projects, urban trees may cut down only a bit of heat by the middle of this century. Because of this, experts believe cities need more solutions. For example, reflective building materials can help. At the same time, ways of cutting greenhouse gases (气体) remain necessary.

Trees are powerful and valuable. They cool cities, improve air quality and make urban life more comfortable. But in a warming world, they work best as part of a larger plan rather than as the only answer.
